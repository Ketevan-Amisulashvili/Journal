Every translation is born between two silences. The first is the silence of the original: what the author left unsaid, yet which can be felt in every line. The second is the translator’s own: the moment when a word has been read but not yet written.

Walter Benjamin wrote that a translation is the original’s “afterlife.”[^1] The metaphor is quoted often, but we rarely weigh it. If translation continues a life, then the original must in some sense die in order to rise again in a new language.

With Georgian poetry the problem is especially acute. The rhyme of our language, its rich verbal system and the music of its vowels do not pass directly into English. The translator has to choose: keep the sound, or keep the sense.[^2]

But perhaps the choice is false. Perhaps a good translation is neither sound nor sense, but the space that remains between them: a palimpsest, where the older text still shows beneath the new.

Every text in this journal is written in such a space. Original and translation stand side by side here, so that readers can judge for themselves what was lost and what was found.

[^1]: Walter Benjamin, “The Task of the Translator” (1923). Benjamin’s word is *Fortleben*, “continued life.”

[^2]: The dilemma is old. Medieval Georgian translators already argued over “Hellenophile” word-for-word translation and freer methods.
